The Homer Oracle

Think of your question. Then throw the die three times: the three numbers point to one line of Homer, and that line is the gods' answer. This is how it was done with a magical papyrus from Roman Egypt, about 1,700 years ago.

First, pray to Apollo
κλῦθι, ἄναξ, ὅς που Λυκίης ἐν πίονι δήμῳ
Hear me, lord, wherever you are, in the rich land of Lycia
εἶς ἢ ἐνὶ Τροίῃ· δύνασαι δὲ σὺ πάντοσ’ ἀκούειν
or in Troy: for you can hear from anywhere
ἀνέρι κηδομένῳ, ὡς νῦν ἐμὲ κῆδος ἱκάνει.
a man in trouble, as trouble has come to me now.
καί μοι τοῦτ’ ἀγόρευσον ἐτήτυμον, ὄφρ’ ἐῢ εἰδῶ,
So tell me this truly, so that I may know it well:
ὅττι μάλιστ’ ἐθέλω καί μοι φίλον ἔπλετο θυμῷ.
the thing I most want, the thing dear to my heart.

The prayer the Oxyrhynchus copy gives for this oracle. It is itself made of lines of Homer: the first three are Glaucus' prayer to Apollo (Iliad 16.514–516); the fourth is Odyssey 1.174; the fifth is Odyssey 18.113, changed to say "I" instead of "you". The papyrus is damaged here, so the wording is partly restored. English: our own translation.

The papyrus's table of days

Straight after the 216 lines, the London papyrus lists the 30 days of the month with the hours when the oracle may be used, under the heading ἡμερομαντίαι καὶ ὧραι, "day-oracles and hours". μὴ χρῶ, "do not use", marks the days when it must not be consulted. Greek months began with the new moon, so counting by the moon is closer to the ancient way; but Roman Egypt also used a calendar of 30-day months, so we cannot be sure which the papyrus meant. Words in [brackets] are torn and restored. Days 24–30 follow Kenyon's printed reading.

How to consult

  1. Check that today is a day when the oracle may be used.
  2. Call on Apollo, god of prophecy: say the prayer, aloud or in your head.
  3. Think of one clear question, and keep it in mind.
  4. Throw the die three times. Each throw gives a number from 1 to 6.
  5. The three numbers, in order, name one of 216 lines of Homer (6 × 6 × 6).
  6. Read the line as the answer to your question. Its meaning is for you to work out, as it was for the ancients.

Your answers

    How do we know?
    • ✔ A real oracle. The list survives on a papyrus roll from Roman Egypt, now in the British Library (Papyrus 121, about the 4th century AD). It begins with 216 single lines from the Iliad and Odyssey, each numbered by three throws of a die. Two more copies survive in part, from Oxyrhynchus and Bologna, and they agree with it.
    • ✔ How to use it. The Oxyrhynchus copy gives the instructions: know the days when the oracle may be used, call on Apollo with the prayer and ask for what you want, then throw the die three times and find the line. The papyrus calls itself Homeromanteion, "Homer oracle".
    • ✔ Homer as scripture. Greeks treated Homer's poems as a source of wisdom, and lines picked at random could be read as messages from the gods.
    • ✔ Pieced together. The start of the London roll is torn into fragments. In 1995 Franco Maltomini fitted them back together, checked them on the original, and used the two other copies to fill gaps. Today 204 lines are known; 8 are lost completely and 4 survive only as scraps. If you throw one of those, the oracle is silent.
    • ✔ A hidden word. The first letters of the first five lines (throws 1-1-1 to 1-1-5) spell ΑΘΗΝΑ, Athena: Ἄνδρ’, Θαρσῶν, Ἦ ῥα, Νίκην, Ἀλλ’. This acrostic shows that the order Maltomini worked out for the torn first column is right.
    • ✔ Another name. The Oxyrhynchus copy says the oracle could also be called "the Scimitar" (a curved sword). No one is sure why; it may echo a spell called "the Sword of Dardanos".
    • ✔ Good days and bad days. The London papyrus says on which days of the month, and at which hours, the oracle may be used. See "Is today a good day?" above.
