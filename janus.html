The Coin of Janus

It is the Kalends of January, the first day of the year, at the bronze gateway of Janus in the Forum. Bring the god a sweet gift, say the old prayer, and toss his coin three times: his face opens a door, Saturn's ship sails on.

1. Bring a sweet gift

Romans gave sweet things at New Year, "so that the year may be sweet".

The god of doors waits for your three tosses.

How to read the coin

  • Janus (heads, capita): the god of doors. A way opens.
  • The ship (navia): Saturn's voyage. Something out of your hands, a journey, or "not yet".
  • Three tosses make eight answers: where the faces and ships fall says when the door opens.

Your answers

    How do we know?
    • ✔ The coin. The aes grave, "heavy bronze": Rome's as, cast about 225-217 BC and weighing around 270 grams. One side shows two-faced Janus with the mark I (one as); the other, a ship's prow with ROMA.
    • ✔ Why a ship? Macrobius and Ovid tell how Saturn sailed to Italy and King Janus welcomed him, and how Janus first cast bronze coins with his own head and, for Saturn, a ship. Roman children tossed coins calling capita aut navia, "heads or ships".
    • ✔ Janus comes first. Romans named Janus first in their prayers. In Ovid's poem he explains why: he keeps the doors, and through him you reach the other gods. The prayer here is real, from Cato's book on farming.
    • ✔ New Year gifts. On the Kalends of January, Romans gave honey, dates, figs and coins, so the year would be sweet.
    • ✔ His gateway. The shrine of Janus in the Forum was a bronze gateway with doors at both ends, open in war and shut in peace.
    • ? The oracle is ours. No source says Romans consulted Janus with his coin. The eight answers, and reading the coin three times, are a modern invention built on his story.

    Sources: Ovid, Fasti book 1; Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.7; Cato, On Farming 134. Translations and answers: our own. Coin sound: Kenney (CC0).
